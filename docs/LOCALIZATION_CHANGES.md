# Notable Translation Changes

> **Spoiler warning:** This document contains substantial story and character spoilers.

This document collects representative examples of how the official English localization differs from the Japanese script and how the mod handles those differences. It is not a complete changelog. More than **25,000** records were changed or re-translated, so the examples below are a small selection meant to show the kinds of changes that recur throughout the game.

The mod has two text variants. **Honorifics** is the canonical translation and keeps confirmed Japanese honorifics and relationship-based forms of address where the source uses them. **No Honorifics** uses the same translation, names, terminology, story information and gameplay text, but replaces those forms of address with English alternatives where needed. Because the examples in this document come from the canonical Honorifics version, revised lines may include forms such as *-sama* or *Onee-sama*.

Each comparison shows the **Japanese** source, the **Official English** localization and the **Revised translation** used by the mod. The selection focuses on changes that affect meaning, characterization, relationships, gameplay information or worldbuilding, along with a smaller number of dialogue examples that show recurring changes in character voice. A missing honorific by itself is not enough to qualify unless the form of address carries a relationship or was replaced with something materially different.

The goal is not to make every line more literal or less colorful. The mod aims to preserve what the Japanese actually says while still using natural English, without adding a second layer of characterization, lore or information on top of the source. The examples below show what that means in practice.

<a name="story-information"></a>

## Story information that disappeared or changed

These are some of the easiest differences to compare because they change concrete information rather than style. In each case, the Japanese gives the player a fact, objective or piece of reasoning that the official English changes or drops.

### Galerius: the final battle loses part of its own logic

Near the end of the story, the characters work out that killing Galerius would let him possess the person who dealt the final blow. The scene repeats this because their plan depends on it.

One line spells out the mechanic directly:

> **Japanese**  
> 殺した者に憑りつく力が  
> その自信の根拠なのか
>
> **Official English**  
> It's some trick you've got there, Galerius.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> So the power to possess whoever kills you is the source of your confidence?

The official line reduces that discovery to “some trick,” so the rule itself disappears. A later line follows the same reasoning:

> **Japanese**  
> …ガレリウス、ようするに  
> アンタを殺さなきゃいいんだろ？
>
> **Official English**  
> We'll see about that.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Galerius, in short, we just have to make sure we don't kill you, right?

And then the same condition is stated again:

> **Japanese**  
> 殺さずに…  
> 大人しくさせてやる
>
> **Official English**  
> Hmph. We'll see about that.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> I'll make him submit... without killing him.

Two separate reminders of the plan become nearly identical lines of generic defiance in English. The fight still happens, but the dialogue no longer explains the precaution the characters are taking.

The same sequence also replaces a concrete objective with a threat:

> **Japanese**  
> 奴の術式を止めましょう  
> アレイン様
>
> **Official English**  
> Come. The decrepit fool won't live to see another day.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Let's stop his spell, Alain-sama.

Here the objective is to stop the spell. The localized line replaces that with a threat to kill the caster, which is especially awkward immediately after the scene has explained why a killing blow is dangerous.

### A route decision lost Alain's reason for making it

Before the final confrontation, Alain hesitates. He thinks the group may already be able to defeat Galerius, but something still bothers him, so he suggests investigating Bisfaine Cathedral:

> **Japanese**  
> このままガレリウスを討つことは  
> できるかもしれないが何か引っかかる  
> アルビオンのビスファイン大聖堂に  
> 向かうことも検討しよう
>
> **Official English**  
> Yes, I would rather not leave this task unfinished if we can avoid it.  
> Let us depart for Albion—or, of course, confront Galerius should we feel so inclined.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> We might be able to defeat Galerius as things stand, but something still bothers me.  
> We should consider heading to Bisfaine Cathedral in Albion as well.

The destination survives in the localization, but Alain's reasoning does not. Instead of voicing a concrete doubt, the line reads as a loose choice between leaving for Albion and fighting Galerius “should we feel so inclined.”

### Two sons were dead, not merely missing

A woman explaining why she refuses to leave her home says exactly what happened to her family:

> **Japanese**  
> 私にゃ三人の息子がいたがね  
> 皆兵士に取られちまった  
> 長男と次男はもう戦死したが  
> 三男はきっとまだ生きてる  
> だから私ゃここを離れられない  
> あの子が戻るまではね
>
> **Official English**  
> All three of my sons got conscripted into the war, right when it started.  
> The older two went into a battle one day and never came back... but my youngest is still out there somewhere.  
> And I'm gonna stay right here until he's home where he belongs.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> I had three sons, you see, but they were all taken away to be soldiers.  
> My eldest and second-born died in battle, yet I'm sure my youngest is still alive.  
> That's why I cannot leave this place—not until my boy comes home.

The woman is not waiting for all three sons. She knows the two older ones died in battle and believes only the youngest may still return. The localization turns those deaths into disappearances and also adds timing that is not in the Japanese. The revised wording keeps some of her older, conversational cadence without assigning her an unrelated English regional dialect.

### Unknown casualties became confirmed dead officers

A military report makes a similar shift from uncertainty to certainty:

> **Japanese**  
> 各騎士団、各兵団再編成が必要だが  
> 後方軍事参謀の生死も不明だ
>
> **Official English**  
> All of our knight orders need some serious restructuring, but we don't have enough officers left alive to do it.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> The knightly orders and military units need reorganizing, but we don't even know whether the rear army's staff officers survived.

The source never says that too few officers survived. Their fate is simply unknown. The localization supplies both a casualty outcome and a new explanation for why the units need reorganizing.

### A temporary appointment became a grand political future

After the Pontifex's death, the Japanese describes a temporary administrative arrangement:

> **Japanese**  
> 教皇が亡くなられ、新体制移行まで  
> ナイジェル様が指導者と定まりました
>
> **Official English**  
> With His Holiness's passing, Lord Nigel has been tasked with leading our nation into a radiant new future.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> With the Pontiff's passing, Nigel-sama has been appointed leader until the transition to the new administration.

Nigel's appointment is temporary while the new administration is being established. The official English drops that limitation and replaces it with a ceremonial line about leading the nation into a radiant future.

<a name="family-relationships"></a>

## Family relationships are part of the story

Several relationship changes are easy to miss if forms of address are treated as decoration. In these scenes, the wording tells us directly what the characters are to one another, and that information is sometimes removed or replaced in English.

### Ramona is explicitly her mother

> **Japanese**  
> あたしも  
> ラモーナ様がお母さんでよかったよ
>
> **Official English**  
> I'm glad you're the one who found us, all those years ago.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Me too. I'm glad you're my mother, Ramona-sama.

The opening `あたしも` ("me too") is part of the response as well. Yunifi explicitly says that she, too, is glad Ramona-sama is her mother. The official line replaces that with a memory about being found years earlier.

### Josef speaks as Alain's adoptive father

> **Japanese**  
> イレニア様から貴方をお預かりした…  
> 養父として申しております
>
> **Official English**  
> I say this not as your advisor, but as the father given care of you by Queen Ilenia.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> I'm speaking as your adoptive father, entrusted with your care by Ilenia-sama.

The official version comes close, but it adds a contrast ("not as your advisor") that isn't in the source, whereas the Japanese simply uses the specific term `養父` (**adoptive father**).

### Gilbert asks whether Ludwig still sees him as a younger brother

> **Japanese**  
> 兄上の方こそどうなんだ  
> もう俺を弟とは思えないのか？
>
> **Official English**  
> ...I would ask the same question of you. If you've truly renounced all that you once were, what of me?
>
> **Revised translation**  
> What about you, Aniue? Can you no longer think of me as your younger brother?

The localized line keeps the tension but removes the question Gilbert is actually asking: does Ludwig still think of him as his younger brother?

### An older sister became fantasy vocabulary

Eltolinde addresses her actual older sister:

> **Japanese**  
> お姉様…様子がおかしいわ  
> 入り口に敵がいないようです
>
> **Official English**  
> Something is odd here, *Ionísa*. I would expect enemies to bar the entrance, but there are none in sight.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Onee-sama... Something's wrong. There don't seem to be any enemies at the entrance.

This is more than the removal of an honorific. A transparent family relationship was replaced with an invented fantasy word. The No Honorifics edition uses an English form of address here rather than restoring *Ionísa*.

<a name="romance-rewritten"></a>

## Romance was often rewritten, but not always in the same direction

The Ring of the Maiden scenes contain some of the clearest additions in the localization. Several lines become more romantic, more exclusive or more explicitly marital than the Japanese. Elsewhere, however, the localization does the opposite and tones down affection that is plainly present.

The mod is not trying to push these scenes in either direction. It keeps the level of intimacy that is actually there in the source.

### Nina was not offered a crown in this line

> **Japanese**  
> だからニーナ、これから  
> ずっと傍で俺と共に戦ってくれないか
>
> **Official English**  
> Hence I would ask you to stay there as my resplendent queen, for the rest of our mortal days.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> So, Nina... Will you keep fighting beside me from now on?

Alain asks Nina to keep fighting at his side. *Resplendent queen* and *the rest of our mortal days* are not present in the Japanese sentence.

### Primm does not accept a marriage proposal here

> **Japanese**  
> 私でよければ、どうか…  
> お傍にいさせてください
>
> **Official English**  
> I would love nothing more than to be your bride, Alain. I accept, a hundred times over.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> If you'll have me, please... let me stay by your side.

Primm asks to stay by Alain's side. The official version turns that into an explicit acceptance of marriage and adds both Alain's name and *a hundred times over*.

### Trust became a direct declaration of love

> **Japanese**  
> 貴女を一番に信頼していて  
> それで…だから
>
> **Official English**  
> I only ask this of you as the one I trust most. The one I love.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> You're the person I trust most, and... that's why...

Alain is struggling to say that Monica is the person he trusts most. The official English finishes the thought for him with a new sentence: *The one I love.*

### Ochlys's awkward admission is about trust

> **Japanese**  
> 剣の腕と心根の優しさは  
> 私も、し…信頼している…
>
> **Official English**  
> I've never once doubted the keenness of your blade, nor the kindness of your heart. I... I care for you deeply, Alain.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> I-I trust your swordsmanship and the kindness in your heart, too...

Ochlys is stumbling over an admission of trust. The localization turns that awkward moment into a different kind of confession.

### A comforting gesture became a kiss

> **Japanese**  
> でも、そのときは私が傷を癒し  
> 撫でてあげますからね…
>
> **Official English**  
> But if you are, I'll be right there to heal your wounds. To kiss them better as well.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> But if that happens, I'll heal your wounds and soothe you with a gentle touch.

Sharon promises to heal Alain and comfort him with a gentle touch. The kiss is an addition.

### Rebuilding Cornia together became marriage

> **Japanese**  
> 必ず生きて  
> 二人でコルニアを再建するわよ
>
> **Official English**  
> We'll get through this, Alain... together. And rebuild Cornia as husband and wife.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> We're going to survive this and rebuild Cornia together, you and I.

The promise is already intimate: survive, then rebuild Cornia together. *As husband and wife* is an addition to this line.

### And sometimes the localization removes affection instead

The same comparison produces the opposite result in Dinah and Yunifi's conversation.

Dinah finally admits what she actually wants:

> **Japanese**  
> …悪かったよ、ユニフィ  
> 私だって…本当は一緒にいたいさ
>
> **Official English**  
> I'm sorry, Yunifi. I wish I could promise that, but the real world's not as pretty as we'd like.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> ...I'm sorry, Yunifi. I want us to be together too. I really do.

The official English changes her admission into a refusal based on how harsh the world is. Immediately afterward, Yunifi responds:

> **Japanese**  
> …！ ダイナありがとう！大好きだよ！
>
> **Official English**  
> ...Thank you, Dinah. You're the best friend a girl could ever ask for.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> ...! Thank you, Dinah! I love you!

`大好きだよ` does not force one particular romantic reading by itself, but it is an open expression of love or deep affection. Replacing it with *best friend* narrows the line in a different direction.

These scenes are a useful counterexample to the idea that the revision simply strips romance out. Sometimes the localization adds it, and sometimes it removes it. The correction goes whichever way the Japanese requires.

<a name="character-voice"></a>

## Character voice: when simple dialogue became a performance

Not every problem is a changed fact. A large part of the rewrite involved ordinary lines that had been expanded into theatrical, pseudo-archaic or unusually witty English. One embellishment may not matter much on its own, but the pattern changes how the cast sounds over the course of the game.

### “That's right” became a miniature legend

> **Japanese**  
> そのとおりだ
>
> **Official English**  
> 'Tis no legend, but truth.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> That's right.

The Japanese is simply a straightforward confirmation. The extra legend language belongs to the localization.

### “Let's keep moving” acquired the winds of fate

> **Japanese**  
> このまま進みましょう
>
> **Official English**  
> The winds of fate blow at our backs.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Let's keep moving.

The source is just as plain here. The winds-of-fate metaphor was added in English.

### A victory line became a philosophy

> **Japanese**  
> 私の勝ちだ！
>
> **Official English**  
> Reason rules the day.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> I win!

The original is simply a victory declaration, and the Japanese line itself ends with an exclamation mark.

### Gilbert was drinking, not making a speech

> **Japanese**  
> ゴク…ゴク…ゴク…ゴクン
>
> **Official English**  
> In that case... pray for me, friends. I do this in the name of knowledge.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Gulp... Gulp... Gulp... Gulp!

The Japanese line is simply the sound of Gilbert drinking, which is the joke. The localization replaces it with a speech about knowledge.

### A musclehead's joke became a completely different boast

> **Japanese**  
> 筋肉は全てを解決する  
> 力こそ正義だ！
>
> **Official English**  
> History is written by the victors...  
> and I am the one who holds the pen!
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Muscles solve everything!  
> Might makes right!

The original is deliberately blunt and silly. The official line replaces the character's muscle obsession with an unrelated metaphor about writing history.

### Firm refusal became a death threat

> **Japanese**  
> これより先はガレリウス様の居城  
> 誰も通すことはできん
>
> **Official English**  
> Emperor Galerius's domain lies beyond this point. Turn around, or die where you stand.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Beyond this point lies Galerius-sama's castle. I cannot let anyone pass.

The guard refuses to let anyone pass. He does not threaten to kill the listener on the spot; that threat was added in English.

### A shop farewell acquired a religious blessing

> **Japanese**  
> またお越しくださいませ
>
> **Official English**  
> May the Father watch over you.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Please come again.

This is ordinary shop language. The religious content belongs entirely to the localization.

These examples are easy to see in isolation, but the larger problem is cumulative. Villagers, mercenaries, soldiers and minor NPCs repeatedly pick up the same elevated vocabulary and theatrical phrasing. The English is often polished, but the polish can make unrelated speakers sound much more alike than they do in Japanese.

<a name="tutorials-and-mechanics"></a>

## Tutorials and mechanics: wording can change how the game is played

In a tactical RPG, a mistranslated condition is not just a style problem. Several tutorials and labels changed targets, thresholds, trigger conditions or the action the player was being told to take.

### A Feathersword counter was reversed

> **Japanese**  
> 「シールドシューター」以外にも  
> 対策は練られるはずだ  
> 「ブレイカー」の攻撃を必中に  
> できれば一網打尽にできそうだな
>
> **Official English**  
> Despite our vulnerability, there exist ways to counteract the Shieldshooter's potency.  
> Granting a Breaker a truestrike attack, for example, could ensure they're defeated in a single strike.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Shieldshooters aren't the only way to counter us.  
> Give a Breaker a truestrike attack, and it could take us all out at once.

The speaker is explaining **another way to counter Featherswords**. The official first sentence makes it sound as though the player is being taught how to counter Shieldshooters instead. The next sentence still mentions a truestrike Breaker, so the lesson becomes internally confusing.

### Yunifi's useful targets changed from scouts to cavalry

> **Japanese**  
> ユニフィの得意な兵種は多く  
> 飛行兵や斥候兵のほかに…  
> 「ソードマスター」にも有効です  
> 作戦を設定して、試してみてください
>
> **Official English**  
> With such power in her hands, Yunifi will prove effective against a great many enemy types.  
> Flying classes, cavalry classes, and even the feared Swordmasters shall fall before her barrage of arrows.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Yunifi is effective against many enemy classes, including flying units and scouts...  
> She's also effective against Swordmasters. Set your tactics and give it a try.

The source says **scouts**, not cavalry, and it explicitly tells the player to configure tactics and test the setup.

### A desert warning stopped telling the player what to prepare

> **Japanese**  
> この先の砂漠へ行く気か？  
> それなら索敵手段を用意していけ
>
> **Official English**  
> Make sure you're ready if you're heading into the desert.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Heading into the desert? Make sure you have a way to scout for enemies.

“Be ready” sounds natural, but it removes the actionable instruction: bring a means of locating enemies.

### A resting unit cannot act at all

> **Japanese**  
> 「休息中」のユニットが戦闘を仕掛けられると  
> 「アクティブスキル」と「パッシブスキル」が封じられ  
> 一切の行動を取ることができません
>
> **Official English**  
> Keep in mind, a resting unit is defenseless against enemy attacks.  
> If an enemy engages a resting unit in battle, that unit will be unable to use Active Skills or Passive Skills.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> If a resting unit is engaged in battle, its Active and Passive Skills are sealed,  
> and it cannot take any action at all.

The source says the unit's Active and Passive Skills are sealed and then adds that it cannot take any action at all. The official tutorial keeps the first part but drops the broader restriction.

### Halving hit rate is not the same as doubling dodge rate

> **Japanese**  
> 地上近接攻撃の命中率を半減
>
> **Official English**  
> 2x dodge rate v. grounded melee
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Halves grounded melee attack accuracy

Those formulas are not equivalent. Halving the attacker's hit rate and doubling the defender's dodge rate can produce different results.

### Boundary conditions matter in a tactics menu

Several tactics labels use `以下` and `以上`, which include equality. The official English used strict inequalities instead.

| Japanese condition | Official English | Revised translation |
|---|---|---|
| HP25％以下 | Target HP is `<25%` | Target HP is `<=25%` |
| HP25％以上 | Target HP is `>25%` | Target HP is `>=25%` |

The same issue appeared across multiple 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% conditions. A unit at exactly the threshold should qualify.

A separate tactics condition had an even simpler numerical error:

> **Japanese:** 敵が３体以下  
> **Official English:** `2 or Fewer` Enemies  
> **Revised translation:** `3 or Fewer` Enemies

### “Another ally” excludes the skill user

A recurring passive trigger says:

> **Japanese**  
> 他の味方がパッシブスキルを使用した際に発動
>
> **Official English**  
> Activates after an ally uses a passive skill.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Activates after another ally uses a passive skill.

The word `他の` matters: the character carrying the skill does not count as the ally who triggers it. This distinction appeared in dozens of related records.

### “Of maximum HP” became “above maximum HP”

> **Japanese**  
> 最大HPの%sまで回復できる
>
> **Official English**  
> Can heal up to %s above maximum HP.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Can heal up to %s of maximum HP.

These describe different caps. The Japanese gives the resulting limit as a percentage **of** maximum HP; the official description reads as though the percentage is an additional amount above the normal maximum.

Gameplay text therefore received the same kind of review as dialogue. A sentence can read perfectly well and still teach the wrong target, threshold, trigger or cap.

<a name="invented-terminology"></a>

## Invented terminology that became part of the English world's lore

Elheim shows a different kind of change. Several descriptive Japanese terms were replaced with newly coined proper nouns. That makes the English terminology sound more elaborate, but it also creates names and lore that the source does not contain.

### Ervélda is the Divine Tree

> **Japanese**  
> 神樹のタリスマン
>
> **Official English**  
> Ervélda's Talisman
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Divine Tree Talisman

`神樹` is the Divine Tree. *Ervélda* is not a reading of those characters. The same replacement appears elsewhere where the Japanese refers to the Divine Tree directly, making a descriptive sacred object look like a separately named being.

### Turenós is simply the priestess

A battle line makes the substitution especially easy to see:

> **Japanese**  
> 巫女の名にかけて…
>
> **Official English**  
> On my honor as *Turenós*.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> As a priestess, I swear...

`巫女` is the ordinary term being used here: priestess. The same invented title appears in longer lines and can obscure other information at the same time. In one story passage, the Japanese says that the speaker's grandmother, who was the priestess at the time, went to the dark elves' underground city **with the speaker's father**. The official English uses *Turenós* and omits the father from the sentence.

### Nasthírion is the Tower of Thorns

> **Japanese**  
> 茨の塔
>
> **Official English**  
> Nasthírion
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Tower of Thorns

The Japanese location name is literally and transparently the Tower of Thorns. The Western proper noun is an additional name, not a romanization of the source.

### The Anemoi layer was not in the Japanese terminology

The same pattern affected a family of wind-related names:

| Japanese | Official English | Revised translation |
|---|---|---|
| 八風神の武具 | Armaments of the Anemoi | Armaments of the Eight Wind Gods |
| 風神の遺跡 | Anemoi Ruins | Wind God Ruins |
| 風門 | Monument to the Anemoi | Wind Gate |
| 古風鎧 | Anemoi Armor | Old-Style Armor |

These Japanese names are not all referring to one proper noun. Treating every `風`-related expression as *Anemoi* flattens distinct phrases into a localized lore system that the original text does not use.

### Ancient Magic Circle is one place, not “The Sigil's Trial”

> **Japanese**  
> 古代の魔法陣　中級１
>
> **Official English**  
> The Sigil's Trial, Moderate 1
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Ancient Magic Circle: Intermediate 1

The quest series is named for the Ancient Magic Circle. The revised titles keep all stages tied to the same named trial site instead of introducing a second location or concept called *The Sigil's Trial*.

Creative terminology is not automatically a problem. What matters is whether the Japanese actually names the thing that way. Where it does not, the mod uses the term found in the source instead of preserving a separate layer of localized lore.

<a name="ordinary-npc-dialogue"></a>

## Ordinary NPC dialogue accumulated invented facts

One-off town conversations are easy to overlook, but they show how much extra characterization and worldbuilding can accumulate in small lines. None of the examples below changes the main plot; each simply gives an NPC information, attitudes or behavior that the Japanese never gave them.

### A nervous customer was not expecting execution

> **Japanese**  
> いや休まるもんも休まらねえだろ！  
> 王様に粗相でもしたら…
>
> **Official English**  
> Ugh, how am I s'posed to relax *now*? One wrong word n' my head'll be decoratin' the walls of Soldraga.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> How am I supposed to relax *now*!? What if I offend the king...?

He is worried about offending the king. The execution imagery is entirely new.

### A dependable daughter became “three times the worker”

> **Japanese**  
> ズボラな私と違って  
> 娘はしっかり者でね
>
> **Official English**  
> I'm not sure what I'd do without my daughter here—she's three times the worker I'll ever be.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Unlike a scatterbrain like me, my daughter really has a good head on her shoulders.

The speaker calls himself a scatterbrain and describes his daughter as level-headed and dependable. The “three times the worker” comparison is a new idea.

### Protecting a town means people can return to work

> **Japanese**  
> 町を守ってくれてありがとう  
> 俺たちも仕事に戻れるよ
>
> **Official English**  
> I appreciate what you did over in town, you know. Here, let me teach you a little something to prove it.
>
> **Revised translation**  
> Thank you for protecting the town. Now we can get back to work.

The Japanese gives a small, ordinary consequence of the town being safe again: people can return to work. The localization replaces it with an offer of training.

### A merchant was surprised by the location, not the customer's species

> **Japanese**  
> こんな地下によく来たね  
> 良かったらゆっくり見ていって
>
> **Official English**  
> Human customers, down here? Odd.  
> I mean... welcome in!
>
> **Revised translation**  
> You've come all the way underground?  
> Feel free to look around.

The surprise is that the customer came this far underground. The Japanese says nothing about humans, so the species reaction is an addition.

None of these lines is important by itself. Taken together, though, they give background characters threats, jokes, religious language and social attitudes that the Japanese script never established.

<a name="natural-english"></a>

## The revised script is not trying to make everyone speak literally

Fidelity here does not mean copying Japanese word order or making dialogue stiff. When the source is short and casual, the English can be short and casual too.

For example:

| Japanese | Official English | Revised translation |
|---|---|---|
| 俺に任せておけ | Courage and strength will see us through. | Leave it to me! |
| 騎兵の力が必要か？ | How can I assist? | Need cavalry? |
| 私を見ておけ！ | Witness the might of an angel. | Watch me! |
| このまま進みましょう | The winds of fate blow at our backs. | Let's keep moving. |

The revised lines are not better because they copy Japanese grammar. They are closer because they preserve the thought the speaker actually chose to express.

Many official lines are perfectly good English sentences. The issue is not whether they sound elegant; it is whether that elegance comes from replacing the original line with a new one.

<a name="selected-terminology"></a>

## Selected terminology corrections

Not every terminology change needs a full example. This table collects a few recurring cases where the official English name points to a different object, group or concept. Minor spelling preferences are omitted.

| Official English | Revised translation | Japanese | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ervélda | Divine Tree | 神樹 | A descriptive sacred tree was turned into a new proper name. |
| Turenós | Priestess | 巫女 | An ordinary religious office became invented fantasy terminology. |
| Nasthírion | Tower of Thorns | 茨の塔 | A transparent location name was replaced by an unrelated proper noun. |
| Forest of the Fae | Forest of Spirits | 精霊の森 | `精霊` is the same spirit terminology used elsewhere, not a separate “fae” category here. |
| Province of the Ashen Blue | Knights' Domain | 騎士団領 | The Japanese names the territory by its political/military function. |
| Salamander Knights | Crimson Dragon Knights | 紅龍騎士団 | The organization is named for a crimson dragon, not a salamander. |
| Witches' Swamp | Witches' Village | 魔女の里 | The source names a settlement, not a swamp. |
| Ancient Dragon Skull | Ancient Dragon Bone | 古代竜の骨 | `骨` is bone; the item is not specifically a skull. |
| The Sigil's Trial | Ancient Magic Circle | 古代の魔法陣 | Separate quest titles were restored to the same named trial location. |
| Angel (race/category) | Winged | 有翼人 | The source distinguishes the Winged people from `天使`, “Angel,” used elsewhere as a title. |

The same approach was used for classes, skills, factions, items, facilities and chapter names. Official names were kept where the Japanese supports them and changed where they point to something different.
